# Conversation warm-up: evidence and implementation

Evaluated on 2026-09-09. This is an optional practice design, not a validated fluency intervention.

## Evidence

The user described Vinh Giang demonstrating a random word ("duck"), associations, and permission to pause. The exact video was not independently located; its details remain user-supplied. An [official Vinh post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/vinhgiang_a-common-question-ive-been-getting-on-social-activity-7156767768221016064-CeE-) discusses asking for thinking time. This is practitioner advice, not experimental evidence.

- [Mehnert (1998), The effects of different lengths of time for planning on second language performance](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263198001041): 31 learners of German with zero, one, five or ten minutes of preparation. Planning benefited oral performance measures, with differences by duration. This does not establish an equivalent effect for a pause of a few seconds, random-word association, or job interviews.
- [de Jong and Perfetti (2011), Fluency Training in the ESL Classroom](https://sites.pitt.edu/~perfetti/PDF/de%20Jong%20Language%20Learning.pdf): 24 ESL students completed three practice sessions with speeches of four, three and two minutes. Improvement persisted in posttests for students repeating their speeches. This supports meaningful repetition; our untimed, shorter warm-up does not replicate the tested protocol.
- [Suzuki and Hanzawa, Massed task repetition is a double-edged sword for fluency development](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263121000358): different repetition schedules produced trade-offs across fluency measures; differences between schedules largely disappeared on a novel-topic delayed test. Narrative tasks and nonrandom group allocation limit application to interviews.

No direct experimental evidence was identified in this bounded search that a random-word association game improves technical interview performance. That is an evidence gap, not proof of ineffectiveness.

## Product decisions

1. Associate three distinct ideas with an everyday word; pause as needed or change the word.
2. Choose one association and develop a short spoken or typed response.
3. Answer a real role-specific interview prompt, using a professional cue and a verified example. Do not force the random word into a technical answer.
4. Answer the existing role-card follow-up, then self-review relevance, clarity, and ability to continue after pausing.
5. Repeat the interview answer with one improvement, try another word, or start the existing role-specific interview lesson.

Pauses are allowed throughout. The suggested speaking duration is guidance, not a countdown, enforced limit, or score. Spoken practice uses a self-reported completion checkbox; there is no microphone capture, pronunciation evaluation, or automatic semantic grading. Distinct/nonempty association checks only gate the exercise structure.

The warm-up stays on the page and does not persist answers, self-review, SRS progress, mastery, or assessments. Starting an interview explicitly posts to the existing lesson creation route with the selected role. Existing canonical content, learner progress, and draft behavior in regular lessons remain unchanged.

## How to judge usefulness

Compare a first response with a revised response for the same prompt: did you finish a relevant main idea, give a concrete reason/example, and recover after a pause? Later, try a different prompt without the warm-up. These are observations and self-reports, not proof that the warm-up caused improvement. A useful change would appear in meaningful answers and unfamiliar follow-ups, not just faster word lists.
